Slide Rule Topic 1

Learn the C and D scales.

These are the core working scales on most slide rules. If you can use C and D well, you can multiply, divide, and read most of the rest of the rule with confidence.

Core idea

One scale moves, one scale reads.

The C scale is the moving scale. The D scale is the fixed working scale. To multiply, line up 1 on C with the first factor on D, then read the second factor on C under the product on D.

  • Use the same idea for division, but reverse the reasoning.
  • Keep the decimal place in your head; the rule shows the digits, not the decimal point.
  • Move slowly at first and say the factors out loud.
Practice

Try this sequence

1. Place 1 on C over 2 on D.
2. Find 3 on C.
3. Read the answer on D. It should land at 6.

Once that feels natural, try 4 x 1.5 and 6 x 1.4. If you can do those quickly, you are ready for the two-decade scales.

Why it works

Logarithms turn multiplication into addition.

Slide rules hide the hard part. The spacing is logarithmic, so equal distances correspond to equal multiplication factors. That is the whole trick.

Next step

Ready to move on?

When C and D feel comfortable, go straight to the two-decade scales. They teach squares and square roots using the same logic.

Worked examples

Multiply: 2 × 3 = 6

Divide: 6 ÷ 3 = 2

Why not addition and subtraction, directly? C and D are logarithmic scales — lining them up adds or subtracts the logarithms of two numbers, which multiplies or divides the numbers themselves. There is no tick mark you can just line up and read x + y off, the way you read x × y. Slide rules exist to speed up multiplication, division, roots, powers, and trig; addition and subtraction were normally just done by hand. That said — there's a disguised-multiplication trick that gets you there anyway. See below.

Party trick: adding with C and D — 2.3 + 4.5 = 6.8

To add x and y, find a multiplier m such that x × m = x + y, which works out to m = y⁄x + 1. The slide rule can't read x + y directly, but it's very good at reading a multiplication — so you look up the ratio, add 1 in your head, and let the rule do the rest.

Trick via Derek Ross's Slide Rule Tricks (2005) — the same author behind the N909-ES simulation this page is built on. His post also describes an addition trick using the L scale, but that one only works on rules where L is printed on the slide. On this particular N909-ES, L is printed on the fixed bottom stator, so that version doesn't apply here.

Tip: if the answer seems too big or too small, you probably need to shift the decimal place by a power of 10.
